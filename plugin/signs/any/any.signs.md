# Signs of AI writing: every language, every model

Generated by `byhand render` from the catalogue; a change here is lost.
A weak sign counts only with 3 or more hits of weak signs in a paragraph or section; one phrase counts once.

- any-ai-self-reference (strong): Says in the text that a language model or a named chatbot wrote it. Instead: Delete it and write as the author.
- any-assistant-closing-offer (strong; document): Keeps the chatbot's sign-off under a document: a hope that it helped, or an offer to do more. Instead: Delete the line; end with the document.
- any-assistant-preamble (strong; email, document, post): Keeps the chatbot's opening line above the text, such as an eager yes and 'here is'. Instead: Delete the line; start with the text itself.
- any-chat-tool-markup (strong): Leaves a chat tool's file or document tags in the text. Instead: Delete the tag.
- any-chatbot-url-tag (strong): Keeps a link's tracking tag that names a chatbot, which shows one found the link if not the text. Instead: Strip the tag and open the page before you cite it.
- any-invented-sources (strong): Cites a book, paper, link or DOI that does not exist or does not say this. Instead: Cite only what you have opened, with the page; otherwise leave the claim out.
- any-bold-overuse (usual): Bolds many phrases in running text, as a sales sheet would. Instead: Bold almost nothing; put the key point first in the sentence.
- any-challenges-formula (usual): Closes on a set piece: for all its strengths the subject faces challenges, then a vague hopeful line. Instead: Name the real problem and what is being done about it, or stop.
- any-circular-argument (usual): Says the same point again in other words, adding nothing new. Instead: Let each paragraph add a fact or a step; cut the ones that do not.
- any-coverage-name-drop (usual): Proves importance by listing who covered the subject, not what they said. Instead: Report what a source says; name it where that helps the reader.
- any-defensive-qualification (usual): Adds caveats nobody asked for about how far a source goes, aimed at a claim no one made. Instead: Limit a claim only where a reader could overreach, and give the finding.
- any-didactic-aside (usual): Stops to tell the reader which part deserves their attention. Instead: State the point; the reader decides what to note.
- any-emoji-markers (usual; email, document): Puts an emoji in front of headings or list items. Instead: Drop it; a heading needs no decoration.
- any-generic-over-specific (usual): Trades a telling detail for praise that would suit anyone. Instead: Keep the detail that fits only this subject: the name, number, date or event.
- any-negative-parallelism (usual): Raises a claim nobody made in order to correct it: not this but that, or not just this. Instead: State the point itself; keep the negation only when someone really holds the view you reject.
- any-overstructured-note (usual; message, email): Breaks a note or a reply into titled sections, as if it were a report. Instead: Write a few plain sentences; use a list only for a real list.
- any-promotional-tone (usual): Reads like a brochure about a place, firm or person. Instead: Describe what is there in neutral words.
- any-significance-inflation (usual): Claims an ordinary fact marks a turning point, a legacy or a wider trend, even one it admits is minor. Instead: State the fact; say what changed only if you can name the change.
- any-source-gap-disclaimer (usual): Says details are scarce in the sources, then writes on as if they were not. Instead: Say plainly what you could not find, and write only what you know.
- any-tacked-on-analysis (usual): Ends a fact on a clause saying what it shows, or has the fact itself highlight or underline something. Instead: Stop at the fact. If its meaning is worth stating, state it separately and name who draws it.
- any-unfilled-placeholder (usual): Leaves a fill-in slot in the text: a bracketed name, a URL stub, a date of XX. Instead: Fill it in or cut it before you send.
- any-vague-authority (usual): Credits a claim to unnamed experts, observers or reports, or makes one source sound like many. Instead: Name the person or document, or drop the claim.
- any-bold-lead-in-list (weak): Starts item after item in a list with a bold label and a colon, or numbers bold paragraph titles. Instead: Write plain items or a sentence; keep labels where a reader scans for them.
- any-closing-summary (weak): Ends a section or the whole text by restating it, often under a heading such as Conclusion. Instead: Stop when the point is made; end on the last new thing.
- any-false-range (weak): Writes 'from X to Y' with two things that sit on no real scale. Instead: Name the items, or keep only the one you mean.
- any-flattering-agreement (weak): Opens by praising the reader or agreeing with them before it answers. Instead: Answer; disagree where the facts do.
- any-letter-boilerplate (weak; email, message): Opens with a stock wish that the message finds the reader well. Instead: Start with the reason you are writing.
- any-over-attribution (weak; document): Narrates what a record lists or identifies where the fact could stand alone. Instead: State the fact; attribute only what someone could dispute.
- any-raw-markdown (weak; email, post, document): Leaves Markdown symbols such as **, ## or --- rules where they will not render. Instead: Format for where the text lands; in mail and plain text, drop the symbols.
- any-rule-of-three (weak): Lists three adjectives or short phrases for rhythm, not because there are three things. Instead: Give the items there really are, even if that is one or two.
- any-stock-headings (weak): Titles sections with two nouns joined by 'and', or a stock label such as Legacy. Instead: Use a heading that says what the section says, or none.
- any-synonym-cycling (weak): Renames one thing several ways to avoid repeating a word. Instead: Repeat the plain word.
- any-table-for-prose (weak): Puts a few facts that read better as a sentence into a small table. Instead: Write the sentence; keep tables for data people compare.
